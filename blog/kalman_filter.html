Slide 03

Weighted Norms & Gaussians

Beamer’s “Weighted Norms and Gaussians” slide links least squares to probabilistic thinking: \[ \|\mathbf{e}\|^2_{\mathbf{M}} = \mathbf{e}^\top \mathbf{M} \mathbf{e}, \quad \mathbf{M} \succ 0. \]

Minimizing a quadratic cost is equivalent to maximizing a Gaussian likelihood. Whenever we write \(\mathbf{x} \sim \mathcal{N}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}, \mathbf{P})\), the density is proportional to \(\exp \left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\| \mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}} \|^2_{\mathbf{P}^{-1}} \right)\).

This dual viewpoint (LS vs probability) underpins every KF derivation: priors and measurement models are just weighted quadratics glued together.

Slide 04

Batch Cost View

The LaTeX “Batch view” describes the cost of fitting an entire trajectory at once: \[ \begin{aligned} J_{\text{batch}} =& \sum_{k=0}^{N-2} \|\mathbf{x}_{k+1} - \mathbf{A}_k \mathbf{x}_k - \mathbf{B}_k \mathbf{u}_k\|^2_{\mathbf{Q}_k^{-1}} \\ &+ \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \|\mathbf{y}_k - \mathbf{C}_k \mathbf{x}_k - \mathbf{D}_k \mathbf{u}_k\|^2_{\mathbf{R}_k^{-1}}. \end{aligned} \]

Minimizing this with respect to every state is equivalent to solving one huge weighted normal equation. Elegant, but not online-friendly.

Slide 05

From Batch to Recursive Thinking

Stack all states into \(\mathbf{X}\) and the batch problem becomes \(\|\mathbf{F}\mathbf{X} - \mathbf{g}\|^2_{\mathbf{W}}\). The Kalman filter avoids ever forming \(\mathbf{F}^\top \mathbf{W} \mathbf{F}\); instead it only keeps \((\hat{\mathbf{x}}_k, \mathbf{P}_k)\) and revises them each time a fresh \(\mathbf{y}_k\) arrives.

Dynamic programming language (“Bell’s theorems”) captures this idea: solve a tiny quadratic at each step instead of a massive one at the end.

Slide 06

One-Step Cost Decomposition

At time \(k\), define local copies \(\zeta \approx \mathbf{x}_k\) and \(\xi \approx \mathbf{x}_{k+1}\). The one-step cost from the LaTeX slide reads

\(V_k(\zeta,\xi) = \|\zeta - \hat{\mathbf{x}}^-_k\|^2_{(\mathbf{P}^-_k)^{-1}} + \|\xi - \mathbf{A}_k \zeta - \mathbf{B}_k \mathbf{u}_k\|^2_{\mathbf{Q}_k^{-1}} + \|\mathbf{y}_k - \mathbf{C}_k \zeta - \mathbf{D}_k \mathbf{u}_k\|^2_{\mathbf{R}_k^{-1}}\).

The three terms encode “stay near the prior”, “respect dynamics”, and “fit the sensor”. That’s all a KF does.

Slide 07

Split into Update & Prediction

The derivation splits the previous cost into two smaller ones:

  • Update: minimize \(V^{\text{upd}}_k(\zeta)\) to blend the prior with the measurement.
  • Prediction: minimize \(V^{\text{pred}}_k(\zeta,\xi)\) to roll the posterior forward.

Conceptually this is Bellman optimality for Gaussian quadratics—solve a tiny problem now, propagate its result, repeat forever.

Slide 08

Bell's Theorem 1 (Gaussian Fusion)

The slide deck spends three frames expanding this idea. The short version:

\(\hat{\zeta} = \mathbf{m}_1 + \underbrace{\mathbf{P}^+ \mathbf{C}^\top \mathbf{R}^{-1}}_{\mathbf{K}} (\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{C}\mathbf{m}_1),\quad \mathbf{P}^+ = (\mathbf{P}_1^{-1} + \mathbf{C}^\top \mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{C})^{-1}.\)

Translation: multiply a prior Gaussian by a measurement Gaussian and you get another Gaussian. The Kalman gain is just the weight of that multiplication.

Slide 09

KF Update via Bell's Theorem

Plugging the prior and measurement definitions into Bell’s theorem yields the textbook formulas:

\(\hat{\mathbf{x}}^+_k = \hat{\mathbf{x}}^-_k + \mathbf{K}_k (\mathbf{y}_k - \mathbf{C}_k \hat{\mathbf{x}}^-_k - \mathbf{D}_k \mathbf{u}_k)\)
\(\mathbf{K}_k = \mathbf{P}^-_k \mathbf{C}_k^\top (\mathbf{C}_k \mathbf{P}^-_k \mathbf{C}_k^\top + \mathbf{R}_k)^{-1}, \quad \mathbf{P}^+_k = (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{K}_k \mathbf{C}_k)\mathbf{P}^-_k.\)

The innovation \(\mathbf{e}_k = \mathbf{y}_k - \mathbf{C}_k \hat{\mathbf{x}}^-_k - \mathbf{D}_k \mathbf{u}_k\) is the “surprise” we correct with.

Slide 10

Bell's Theorem 2 → Prediction

Linear functions of Gaussians remain Gaussian. Apply this to the dynamics:

\(\hat{\mathbf{x}}^-_{k+1} = \mathbf{A}_k \hat{\mathbf{x}}^+_k + \mathbf{B}_k \mathbf{u}_k,\quad \mathbf{P}^-_{k+1} = \mathbf{A}_k \mathbf{P}^+_k \mathbf{A}_k^\top + \mathbf{Q}_k.\)

Mean marches through the model, covariance balloons via \(\mathbf{Q}_k\).

Slide 11

Full Recursive Algorithm

The final LaTeX slide summarizes the loop:

  1. Predict \((\hat{\mathbf{x}}^-_{k+1}, \mathbf{P}^-_{k+1})\).
  2. Compute innovation covariance \(\mathbf{S}_{k+1}\) and Kalman gain \(\mathbf{K}_{k+1}\).
  3. Update state, then shrink covariance via \((\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{K}_{k+1}\mathbf{C}_{k+1})\).

Everything else in the KF universe—smoothers, EKFs, UKFs—is just a riff on this loop.

Slide 13

Kalman Gain Playground (1D motion)

Inspired by the introductory plots in the notebook, this chart shows a gliding object, its noisy GPS-like measurements, and the filtered estimate. Vary \(Q\) (model trust) and \(R\) (sensor trust).

1D Kalman Filter Equations:

\(\text{Predict: } \hat{x}_k^- = \hat{x}_{k-1}^+, \quad P_k^- = P_{k-1}^+ + Q\)
\(\text{Update: } K_k = \frac{P_k^-}{P_k^- + R}, \quad \hat{x}_k^+ = \hat{x}_k^- + K_k(z_k - \hat{x}_k^-), \quad P_k^+ = (1-K_k)P_k^-\)

When \(Q \ll R\), the gain \(K_k \to 0\) (trust model). When \(R \ll Q\), \(K_k \to 1\) (trust measurement).

Crank it up to admit that your kinematics model drifts (e.g., uneven throttle).

Higher means the sensor is flaky; the filter leans on the prediction instead.

Slide 16

Enter the Extended Kalman Filter

Chapter 11 in the notebook introduces the EKF. The recipe: linearize the nonlinear model at the current estimate, then apply the linear Kalman filter equations to that local approximation. Two Jacobians appear:

  • \(\mathbf{F}_k = \partial f / \partial \mathbf{x}\): linearizes the process model.
  • \(\mathbf{H}_k = \partial h / \partial \mathbf{x}\): linearizes the measurement model.

The EKF examples—radar tracking and a steering robot—are embedded below with interactive linearization games so you can “feel” how the Jacobians change with the operating point.

Slide 17

Linearization Playground

The EKF linearizes nonlinear functions. Drag the operating point \(x\) to watch the tangent line track the curve \(f(x) = x^2 - 2x\), mimicking the figure from Extended Kalman Filters.

First-Order Taylor Expansion:

\(f(x) \approx f(x_0) + f'(x_0)(x - x_0)\)
\(\text{For } f(x) = x^2 - 2x: \quad f'(x) = 2x - 2\)

At operating point \(x_0\), the tangent line has slope \(f'(x_0) = 2x_0 - 2\). The EKF uses this linearization at each step.

EKFs recompute the slope at every step to keep the linear approximation trustworthy.

Slide 18

Radar EKF - Part 1: States

A ground radar tracks an aircraft with state \(\mathbf{x} = [x, \dot{x}, h]^\top\). The radar measures only slant range.

Nonlinear Measurement Model:

\(z_k = h(\mathbf{x}_k) + v_k = \sqrt{x_k^2 + h_k^2} + v_k, \quad v_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, R)\)
\(\mathbf{H}_k = \nabla h = \begin{bmatrix}\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2 + h^2}} & 0 & \frac{h}{\sqrt{x^2 + h^2}}\end{bmatrix}\)

The Jacobian \(\mathbf{H}_k\) is recomputed at each step using the predicted state \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_k^-\).

Higher values assume more acceleration uncertainty.

Simulates radar precision. Large noise forces the EKF to trust the dynamics.

Nonlinear Robot EKF (Range + Bearing)

The notebook’s second EKF example tracks a wheeled robot with steering inputs while a radar at the origin reports noisy range/bearing. Tune the noise terms to see the EKF follow curved motion.

Bicycle Model (Nonlinear Process):

\(\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \begin{bmatrix}x_k + v\cos(\theta_k)\Delta t\\ y_k + v\sin(\theta_k)\Delta t\\ \theta_k + \frac{v}{L}\tan(\delta)\Delta t\end{bmatrix}\)
\(\mathbf{F}_k = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & -v\sin(\theta_k)\Delta t\\ 0 & 1 & v\cos(\theta_k)\Delta t\\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}\)

Nonlinear Range-Bearing Measurements:

\(\mathbf{z}_k = \begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{x_k^2 + y_k^2}\\ \arctan2(y_k, x_k)\end{bmatrix} + \mathbf{v}_k, \quad \mathbf{H}_k = \begin{bmatrix}\frac{x}{r} & \frac{y}{r} & 0\\ -\frac{y}{r^2} & \frac{x}{r^2} & 0\end{bmatrix}\)

Both process and measurement models are nonlinear, requiring Jacobians \(\mathbf{F}_k\) and \(\mathbf{H}_k\) at each step.
